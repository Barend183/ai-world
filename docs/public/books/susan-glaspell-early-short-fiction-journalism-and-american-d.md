# Susan Glaspell: Early Short Fiction, Journalism, and American Drama

## Introduction
Susan Keating Glaspell (1876–1948) was an American playwright, novelist, journalist, and short story writer. A key figure in early twentieth-century American literature and drama, Glaspell co-founded the **Provincetown Players**, America's first modern experimental theatre company, alongside her husband George Cram Cook. Though widely celebrated in her lifetime for her fifty short stories, nine novels, and twelve plays—including her Pulitzer Prize-winning play *Alison's House* (1930)—Glaspell's legacy fell into relative obscurity following her death before undergoing a major critical revival in the late 20th and early 21st centuries.

## Early Life and Journalism Career
Born in Davenport, Iowa, on July 1, 1876, Glaspell was raised on a rural homestead near the Mississippi River. She demonstrated exceptional academic talent and began earning a salary as a journalist for local newspapers at age eighteen. 

After graduating from Drake University in 1899 with a degree in philosophy—where she excelled in collegiate debate—she became a full-time reporter for the *Des Moines Daily News*. Notably assigned to cover the state legislature and high-profile criminal trials, Glaspell gained intimate insight into social injustice, gender dynamics, and legal proceedings. Her coverage of a 1900 murder case involving a woman accused of killing her abusive husband deeply affected her and later provided the inspiration for her most famous one-act play, *Trifles* (1916), and its short story adaptation, *A Jury of Her Peers* (1917).

## Short Fiction and Literary Success
At age twenty-four, Glaspell resigned from journalism to dedicate herself to fiction. The early 1900s marked a golden era for American short story periodicals, and Glaspell found widespread commercial and critical success. Her work appeared in leading magazines of the day, including *Harper's*, *Munsey's*, *Ladies' Home Journal*, and *Woman's Home Companion*.

Nineteen of her early short stories published between 1898 and 1916 were collected in *The Rules of the Institution and Other Stories*. Her stories frequently explored contemporary social themes, including female autonomy, ethics, provincial Midwestern life, and moral dissent.

In addition to short fiction, Glaspell gained national acclaim with her best-selling debut novel, *The Glory of the Conquered* (1909), followed by *The Visioning* (1911) and *Fidelity* (1915).

## The Provincetown Players and Modern American Theatre
In 1913, Glaspell married George Cram Cook and moved to Greenwich Village, where they immersed themselves in radical artistic and political circles alongside figures like Upton Sinclair, Emma Goldman, and John Reed. 

In the summer of 1915, in Provincetown, Massachusetts, Glaspell, Cook, and fellow artists established the **Provincetown Players** on a converted fishing wharf. Dedicated to producing innovative, non-commercial American plays reflecting real contemporary issues, the group revolutionized American theatre. Glaspell was instrumental in discovering playwright Eugene O'Neill and produced groundbreaking original drama for the company, including:
- ***Trifles* (1916)**: A concise, powerful exploration of subtle domestic oppression, female solidarity, and justice, widely regarded as a masterpiece of modern American drama.
- ***Inheritors* (1921)**: One of the earliest modern historical dramas examining academic freedom and pioneer history across generations.
- ***The Verge* (1921)**: An iconic work of American expressionist drama.

Glaspell also performed in many of the company's productions to critical acclaim, described by French director Jacques Copeau as 'a truly great actress.'

## Legacy and Critical Revival
Though her work went out of print for several decades after her death in 1948, feminist literary scholarship and theatre history since the late 20th century have firmly restored Glaspell to her rightful place in the American literary canon. Today, she is recognized as a pioneering voice in feminist literature and one of the founding figures of modern American drama.
