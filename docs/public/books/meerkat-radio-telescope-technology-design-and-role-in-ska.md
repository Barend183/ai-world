The MeerKAT radio telescope, located 90 km outside Carnarvon in South Africa's Northern Cape, is a highly advanced instrument serving as a precursor to the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) telescope. It will be integrated into the mid-frequency component of SKA Phase 1. The name 'MeerKAT' evolved from 'Karoo Array Telescope' (KAT) with an increased budget allowing for 'more of KAT' (64 receptors instead of 20), also referencing the beloved Karoo mammal, the meerkat (Suricata suricatta).

**Technical Design and Components:**
MeerKAT consists of an array of 64 interlinked receptors. Each receptor is a complete antenna structure, comprising a main reflector, sub-reflector, receivers, digitisers, and other electronics. The configuration of these receptors is optimized for scientific objectives, with 48 concentrated in a 1 km diameter core and a maximum baseline of 8 km between any two receptors.

Each receptor has three main components:
1.  **Antenna Positioner:** This is a steerable dish on a pedestal, made up of a 13.5 m effective diameter main reflector and a 3.8 m diameter sub-reflector. It utilizes an 'Offset Gregorian' optical layout, which eliminates struts that could block or interrupt incoming electromagnetic signals. This design ensures excellent optical performance, sensitivity, imaging quality, and good rejection of unwanted radio frequency interference (RFI) from satellites and terrestrial transmitters. It also allows for the installation of multiple receiver systems.
    *   The combined surface accuracy of the two reflectors is exceptionally high, with a deviation of no more than 0.6 mm RMS from the ideal shape. The main reflector is constructed from 40 aluminium panels mounted on a steel support framework.
    *   The entire structure (pedestal, yoke, and reflectors) stands 19.5 m tall and weighs 42 tons. The pedestal houses the antenna's pointing control system, which allows precise horizontal rotation (azimuth range from -185 to +275 degrees) and observation elevation (15 to 88 degrees). The antenna can point with an accuracy of 5 arcseconds under ideal conditions and 25 arcseconds during normal operations.
2.  **Receivers:** Mounted on a rotating support structure called a receiver indexer, each receptor can accommodate up to four receivers and digitisers. The indexer automatically moves the appropriate receiver into the antenna's focus position based on the desired observation frequency. The primary function of the receiver is to capture electromagnetic radiation and convert it into a voltage signal. This signal is then amplified by cryogenic receivers, which are designed to add very little noise to the signal. The initial receivers are L-Band and UHF Band.
3.  **Digitisers:** Four digitisers are mounted close to their associated receivers on the indexer. Their role is to convert the analog radio frequency (RF) voltage signal from the receiver into digital signals using an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC). For instance, the L-band digitiser samples at an astounding rate of 1,712 million samples per second, generating an immense amount of data.

**Signal Processing and Data Flow:**
Once converted to digital data, the information is transmitted via buried fibre optic cables to the correlator, located within the Karoo Array Processor Building (KAPB) at the Losberg site complex. A total of 170 km of fibre optic cables connect the receptors to the KAPB, buried 1 m underground for thermal stability. Inside the KAPB, signals undergo various digital processing stages:
*   **Correlation:** This process combines all the signals from all 64 receptors to form a cohesive image of the observed area of the sky.
*   **Beam-forming:** Signals from all receptors are coherently added to create multiple narrow, high-sensitivity beams, particularly useful for pulsar science.
Science data products are archived at the KAPB, with a portion transferred to Cape Town via fibre connection for additional storage and potential reprocessing.

**Synchronization and Control:**
Precise timing is critical for radio astronomy. Time and frequency reference signals are distributed via buried optical fibres to every digitiser, ensuring all receptors are perfectly synchronized to a single clock. A comprehensive control and monitoring system, utilizing over 150,000 internal sensors, oversees the telescope's health, monitors environmental conditions, power consumption, and manages operational control as directed by operators.
